# Design notes

This document covers the architecture, the modelling decisions, and the
reasoning behind each one. Numbers quoted here come from
[`benchmarks/results.md`](../benchmarks/results.md), regenerated by
`python benchmarks/run_benchmarks.py`.

---

## 1. Architecture

```
datagen ──► classify ──┐
    │                  │
    │                  ▼
    │            backtest (rolling origin)  ──► per-quadrant aggregation ──► plots
    │                  ▲
    │      ┌───────────┼───────────┐
    │  baselines     ets      intermittent      ml (global GBT)
    │
    └──► hierarchy (S matrix) ──► reconcile (BU / TD / OLS / MinT)
```

Two forecaster contracts, deliberately kept apart:

| contract | interface | who implements it |
|---|---|---|
| local | `fit(y) -> self`, `predict(h) -> (h,)` | baselines, ETS family, Croston family |
| global | `fit_panel(P) -> self`, `predict_panel(h) -> (n, h)` | feature-based gradient boosting |

The backtester dispatches on `is_global`. This is not ceremony. The most
common way a forecasting benchmark ends up wrong is a global model wired into
a loop written for local models, so that predicting series *A* at origin *t*
has already consumed series *B*'s future. Keeping the two contracts separate
puts the cut-off boundary in exactly one place, and the leakage tests in
`tests/test_ml_and_backtest.py` assert against it directly: scramble everything
after the origin, and the design matrix must not move by a single float.

### Why the recursions are hand-written

`statsmodels` is not a dependency. Every state-space recursion in `ets.py` and
every intermittent estimator in `intermittent.py` is written from the published
equations against numpy, with parameters fitted by `scipy.optimize.minimize`
(L-BFGS-B, box-constrained, multi-start). The payoff is not purity. It is that
the initialisation rule, the damping, and the multiplicative guard are all
visible and individually testable -- and those three things, not the smoothing
equations, are where these models actually fail in a live estate.

## 2. Why WAPE, and why not MAPE

MAPE is the default in most planning systems and it is the wrong metric for
most of an assortment.

**It is undefined on a zero actual.** In this benchmark 36.4% of all
bottom-level cells are zero, and in the lumpy quadrant it is 74.8% of weeks.
Any MAPE implementation either drops those periods -- silently evaluating on a
biased subsample of exactly the periods the model found easy -- or floors the
denominator, which invents an error scale out of nothing.

**It is asymmetric in a way that rewards under-forecasting.** The penalty for
forecasting 0 when actual is 10 is capped at 100%; forecasting 10 when actual
is 1 costs 900%. A planner optimising reported MAPE learns to forecast low.
That is not an abstract concern -- it is a direct route to a service-level
problem that the accuracy dashboard will show as an improvement.

**It weights every SKU equally.** A part that sells 2 units a year gets the
same vote as one that sells 200,000.

WAPE (`sum|error| / sum|actual|`, the MAD/Mean ratio) fixes all three. It is
defined whenever the window contains any demand, it is volume-weighted by
construction, and it has a free interpretation that makes it readable without a
baseline column: **forecasting zero always scores exactly 1.0**. Anything above
1.0 is worse than giving up. That property is asserted as a test
(`test_zero_forecast_gives_wape_of_exactly_one`) and it is why `zero` is in the
model zoo.

The one place WAPE is honestly undefined is a window with no demand at all.
This library returns `NaN` there rather than 0 or infinity, and the pooled
aggregations sum numerators and denominators rather than averaging per-series
ratios -- so a dead SKU contributes nothing instead of contributing a fake
perfect score.

**Pooling matters as much as the metric.** `by_quadrant()` pools
`sum|e| / sum|a|` across every series and window in the group. Averaging
per-series WAPE would give the 2%-of-volume lumpy tail the same influence as
the 52%-of-volume smooth head. Both views are legitimate; they answer different
questions, and mixing them up is how a "17% accuracy improvement" turns out to
be entirely in items nobody stocks.

## 3. Why MASE needs a seasonal naive denominator

MASE scales the mean absolute error by the in-sample MAE of a benchmark
method. Which benchmark is not a detail.

With a naive-1 denominator (`mean|y_t - y_{t-1}|`), a seasonal series makes the
denominator large, because consecutive periods differ by the whole seasonal
swing. Any model that merely reproduces the seasonal pattern then scores well
below 1 and appears skilful while having added nothing beyond what a seasonal
index would give you.

With a seasonal denominator (`mean|y_t - y_{t-m}|`), MASE answers the question
that matters: *did this model beat the free seasonal forecast?* On the
benchmark panel the seasonal naive method itself scores MASE 1.232 out of
sample -- above 1, because the in-sample denominator is estimated on a longer
and quieter stretch of history than the test window. That is normal and it is
why MASE is a ranking device, not an absolute grade.

`seasonal_naive_scale` returns `NaN` rather than zero when the seasonal
differences vanish (a perfectly periodic series), and `mase` propagates the
`NaN` instead of dividing by a floor. A metric that silently invents a
denominator is worse than a metric that admits it cannot be computed.

Denominators are recomputed **per rolling window from the training portion**.
Computing them once on the full series leaks test-period volatility into the
scale and shifts the ranking.

## 4. Forecast Value Add, and the baselines that must be beaten

Every result is reported against `snaive[m=52]` as Forecast Value Add:
`1 - metric_model / metric_baseline`. This is the only number that answers the
question a planning organisation actually has, which is not "how accurate is
the model" but "is this worth the maintenance".

On the benchmark panel the best method overall (`gbt_median`, WAPE 0.3976)
delivers **FVA 0.250 against seasonal naive** (WAPE 0.5304). A quarter of the
error, removed. That is a real result, and it is also a sobering one: the
entire modelling stack -- optimised ETS, three intermittent estimators, a
gradient-boosted global model with 30-odd engineered features -- buys 25%
against a method that is four lines of numpy.

The zoo deliberately includes methods that are meant to lose:

- `naive` and `drift` because they occasionally do not lose, and when they win
  it tells you something has broken structurally.
- `mean` and `zero` because they bound the problem. `zero` scoring WAPE 1.0 by
  construction turns WAPE into a self-normalising scale.

## 5. The lumpy quadrant, and what point accuracy cannot tell you

The single most useful finding in this repository is uncomfortable: **on lumpy
demand, nothing beats forecasting zero.**

| quadrant | best method | WAPE | seasonal naive |
|---|---|---|---|
| smooth | `hw_mul[m=52]` | 0.3044 | 0.4586 |
| erratic | `hw_mul[m=52]` | 0.4296 | 0.5856 |
| intermittent | `gbt_median` | 0.3070 | 0.3926 |
| lumpy | `zero` | 1.0000 | 1.4175 |

Every real method on the lumpy quadrant scores above 1.0 -- `gbt_median` gets
closest at 1.013, and the classical smoothers land between 1.27 and 1.42. This
is not a bug in the methods. It is a property of absolute-error scoring on a
series that is zero three weeks in four: the error-minimising constant forecast
is the *median*, and the median is zero.

The correct conclusion is not "forecast zero for lumpy items". It is that
**point accuracy is the wrong objective for that quadrant.** Nobody stocks to a
point forecast of a lumpy part; they stock to a service level, which is a
question about the upper tail of the lead-time demand distribution. That is why
this library carries quantile forecasts and pinball loss alongside the point
metrics, and why the roadmap item that matters most is evaluating the lumpy
tail on fill rate and holding cost rather than on WAPE at all.

It is also why the benchmark reports per-quadrant results in the first place.
Pooled over the whole panel, `gbt_median` looks like a clean win. Split by
quadrant, the honest answer is a routing rule with four different branches, one
of which is "stop using a point forecast here".

## 6. Squared error vs absolute error: the loss *is* the decision

The two gradient boosting entries are the same model with a different training
loss, and they behave very differently:

| model | loss | fits | WAPE | MASE | bias% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `gbt_median` | absolute error | conditional median | 0.3976 | 0.822 | -0.147 |
| `gbt_mean` | squared error | conditional mean | 0.4315 | 1.264 | -0.023 |

`gbt_median` wins on both absolute-error metrics -- as it must, because it is
trained on the same loss the metric measures. It also carries a **-14.7%
systematic bias**: it under-forecasts total volume by nearly a sixth.
`gbt_mean` is nearly unbiased (-2.3%) and loses on WAPE.

For a replenishment system, that bias is not a rounding detail. Feeding a
median forecast into a safety-stock calculation that assumes an unbiased mean
produces a structural stock shortfall that no amount of safety stock tuning
will fix, because the error is in the location parameter, not the spread.

The practical rule this benchmark supports: **pick the loss that matches the
decision, then report the metric that matches the loss, and never let the
accuracy team and the inventory team optimise different ones.** A forecast
scored on WAPE and consumed as a mean is a well-run process producing a
guaranteed error.

## 7. Intermittent demand: three estimators, three different reasons

`examples/04_intermittent_obsolescence.py` demonstrates both properties
numerically.

**Croston (1972)** splits demand into size and interval processes and smooths
each separately at demand epochs only. This fixes the fundamental problem with
running SES on a raw intermittent series -- the estimate collapses toward zero
between demands, so the replenishment signal is strongest immediately *after*
an order, when it should be weakest.

**SBA (Syntetos & Boylan, 2005)** corrects Croston's inversion bias. Because
`E[z/p] != E[z]/E[p]`, Croston's ratio estimator is biased high by roughly
`1/(1 - alpha/2)`, and SBA applies the deflator. The example measures this over
60 seeded replications: at alpha 0.05 Croston's mean error is +0.065 units,
rising to +0.901 at alpha 0.30, while SBA stays within +/-0.11 throughout.

**TSB (Teunter, Syntetos & Babai, 2011)** replaces the interval process with a
demand *probability* updated every period. This is the obsolescence fix, and it
is the reason to choose TSB -- not point accuracy. On a part with 156 weeks of
demand followed by a year of silence:

| weeks dead | croston | sba | tsb |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5.573 | 5.155 | 1.967 |
| 13 | 5.573 | 5.155 | 0.500 |
| 26 | 5.573 | 5.155 | 0.127 |
| 52 | 5.573 | 5.155 | 0.008 |

Croston and SBA are frozen at their last rate for the entire year, because
neither updates on a zero period. In a live system they will keep proposing
replenishment for a discontinued part until somebody manually kills the item
master record. TSB sheds 99.6% of its estimate over the same window.

Note the cost, visible in the same table: TSB is already at 1.967 rather than
the long-run rate of about 5.5 at week 0, because the live series happens to
end on a run of zeros. Responsiveness to silence and a noisier level estimate
are the same property, not two.

## 8. Holt-Winters multiplicative: a model most of the assortment cannot use

The multiplicative seasonal recursion divides by the seasonal index. A zero
demand week drives an index toward zero, the next division explodes, and the
forecast leaves the solar system. In an early run of this benchmark, before the
guard was added, `hw_mul` scored WAPE **365** on the intermittent quadrant.

**62 of the 96 series (65%)** in this panel contain at least one zero week in
the first training window and therefore cannot support a multiplicative model
at all.

The implementation detects this up front and fits the additive form instead,
setting `degenerate_ = True`. Two reasons for doing it that way rather than
clamping the recursion:

1. A clamped recursion is no longer the published model, and quietly
   substituting a different one is how a library becomes untrustworthy.
2. The flag is a *countable* diagnostic. "65% of your assortment cannot use the
   seasonal model your engine defaults to" is an actionable statement; a
   silently clamped forecast is not.

The fallback is why `hw_mul` and `hw_add` post identical numbers on the
intermittent and lumpy quadrants (0.4072 and 1.4164 respectively) -- every
series in those quadrants fell back. They differ only where multiplicative
seasonality is actually defined, and there it wins: 0.3044 vs 0.3239 on smooth,
0.4296 vs 0.4742 on erratic.

## 9. Hierarchical reconciliation and the coherency requirement

A planning organisation does not consume one forecast. Finance consumes the
total, category management consumes product level, the DC network consumes
region, replenishment consumes the SKU x region x channel cell. Produced
independently, those numbers do not add up, and the first thing that happens in
the S&OP meeting is an argument about whose number is wrong instead of a
decision.

On this panel the independent base forecasts miss coherency by **about 6,000 units** (5.99e+03)
at the worst node. Every reconciliation method drives that to exactly zero,
because they all take the form `y~ = S G y^` and every `G` implemented here
satisfies `S G S = S`. That identity is asserted directly in
`tests/test_hierarchy.py` -- feed in an already-coherent set and it must come
back unchanged.

Results by level, WAPE:

| method | total | product | product x region | bottom |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| base (incoherent) | 0.1175 | 0.3455 | 0.4025 | 0.4399 |
| bottom_up | 0.1347 | 0.3460 | 0.4066 | 0.4399 |
| top_down | 0.1175 | 0.4456 | 0.5561 | 0.6453 |
| ols | 0.1257 | 0.3466 | 0.4107 | 0.4530 |
| **mint** | 0.1268 | **0.3280** | **0.3880** | **0.4229** |

Four things worth reading out of that table:

**Coherency is not free at the top.** Bottom-up degrades total-level WAPE from
0.1175 to 0.1347, because summing 96 noisy cells accumulates their errors while
the direct total forecast gets to exploit aggregation smoothing. Anybody who
tells you bottom-up is obviously right has not measured the top level.

**Top-down is the worst at the bottom by a wide margin** (0.6453 vs 0.4399) and
exactly ties the base forecast at the total, which is definitional -- it only
ever uses the top-level number. Historical proportions carry no information
about which cell is currently growing. It survives in practice because it is
the only method a finance process can audit in a spreadsheet, and that is a
real constraint, not a stupid one.

**MinT is the best of the four reconciliation methods at five of the eight
levels** -- product, all three crossed levels, and the bottom (0.4229, a 3.9%
improvement on the incoherent base forecast *and* exact coherency). OLS takes
region and channel; top-down ties the base at the total by definition. MinT
gets there by estimating the error covariance across nodes instead of assuming
a structure for it.

**The shrinkage is doing real work.** The fitted Schafer-Strimmer intensity was
**0.428** -- almost halfway to the diagonal. With 180 nodes and 104 usable
residual observations the sample covariance is rank-deficient by construction;
inverting it produces reconciliation weights that are noise. Shrinkage with an
analytic intensity and no tuning parameter is what makes MinT deployable rather
than a paper result, and `tests/test_hierarchy.py` asserts the adaptive
behaviour: the intensity rises as the residual sample shrinks, and the shrunk
matrix is full rank when the sample one is not.

One deliberate departure from the papers: reconciled bottom-level forecasts are
clipped at zero and re-aggregated. Unconstrained MinT will happily hand
replenishment a negative cell. A negative demand plan is worse than an
incoherent one.

**Grouped, not strictly hierarchical.** Product, region and channel are
crossed, so the structure is a *grouped* time series with 180 nodes over 96
bottom cells. There is no unique parent for a bottom cell and top-down needs an
arbitrary choice of disaggregation path -- which is exactly the case MinT was
formulated to handle, and the normal shape of a commercial hierarchy.

## 10. Feature construction and the leakage discipline

The global model is a *direct* multi-horizon forecaster: horizon is an input
feature and the target is `y[t+h]`, so there is no recursive error compounding
and no separate model per horizon.

Feature groups: lags (0-26), causal rolling means (4-52) and standard
deviations, zero-share windows, mean non-zero size, periods since last demand,
running ADI and CV², a short-run slope, the promotion flag at the origin and at
the target, the aligned seasonal lag `y[t+h-m]` and its ratio to the recent
level, calendar harmonics of the target period, and integer-coded product /
region / channel as categorical splits.

Three rules make it leakage-safe:

1. Every origin-indexed feature is a function of `y[..t]` only. There is no
   `.shift()` on a full-history frame anywhere in the file, because that is
   where leakage usually gets in.
2. Horizon-dependent features are constructed explicitly and each one is either
   in the past (`y[t+h-52]` with `h <= 52`) or genuinely known in advance.
3. Training origins are capped at `t + h <= train_end - 1`.

**Known-future covariates are treated as known.** A promotion calendar is
committed weeks ahead of execution, so the target period's promo flag is a
legitimate feature. Pretending otherwise understates what a feature model can
do in a real planning cycle; using an *unknown* future covariate the same way
overstates it. The distinction is the difference between a model that works in
production and one that only works in a notebook.

## 11. Quantile forecasts and what coverage tells you

Three separate models are fitted with `HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile")`
at tau in {0.5, 0.9, 0.95} and scored with pinball loss:

| quantile | pinball loss | empirical coverage |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 94.713 | 0.653 |
| 0.9 | 71.713 | 0.894 |
| 0.95 | 61.249 | 0.930 |

The upper quantiles are close to nominal, which is the useful result: a q95
forecast that actually covers 93% of outcomes is a usable safety-stock input.

The q50 row reads as badly over-covering at 0.653, and the reason is
instructive rather than a defect. Coverage is `P(actual <= forecast)`, forecasts
are clipped at zero, and 36% of cells *are* zero -- so every zero actual counts
as covered by any non-negative forecast. On a distribution with an atom at zero
the empirical coverage of the median is not 0.5 and should not be expected to
be. This is another symptom of the same thing the lumpy quadrant shows:
continuous-distribution intuitions do not transfer to intermittent demand.

Independent per-quantile fits can also cross (q90 below q50). The benchmark
measures how often -- a test asserts non-crossing on more than 90% of cells --
rather than enforcing monotonicity by sorting, because the crossing rate is
itself a diagnostic of how much the model is extrapolating.

## 12. Testing strategy

110 tests, stdlib `unittest`, no network, no fixtures, full suite in about 10
seconds. The design principle is that a test should assert a **property from
the literature**, not that the code returns an array of the right shape:

- SBA is exactly Croston deflated by `1 - alpha/2`.
- TSB decays on a discontinued item while Croston is bit-identically frozen.
- Every reconciliation `G` satisfies `S G S = S` (coherent input, unchanged
  output).
- Shrinkage intensity is interior when a common factor exists, rises as the
  sample shrinks, and produces a full-rank matrix from a rank-deficient sample.
- Pinball loss is minimised at the true quantile of the generating
  distribution, over four perturbations either side.
- Forecasting zero yields WAPE exactly 1.0.
- Feature construction is invariant to scrambling everything after the origin.
- A global model refitted on a training window whose future has been replaced
  with garbage produces a bit-identical forecast.

The last two are the ones worth stealing.

## 13. Known limitations

See the README's *Limitations* section. The short version: synthetic data
generated by a process this library also knows how to model is a friendly
world, single seeds are single seeds, there is no statistical significance
testing across windows, the lumpy quadrant is evaluated on the wrong objective,
and the entire cost side of the problem -- which is what makes a forecast worth
anything -- is out of scope.
